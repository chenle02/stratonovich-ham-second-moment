\begin{proof}
  Zero noise is trivial; omit any zero $\sigma_i$.
  Replacing $(\sigma_i,W_i^\epsilon)$ by
  $(|\sigma_i|,\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_i)W_i^\epsilon)$ leaves the
  potential unchanged and preserves the joint law of the independent
  real Gaussian fields. Thus, we may assume $\sigma_i>0$.
  We first work with finitely many Gaussian coordinates of each field and
  a bounded spatial Fourier band. Denote their common phase Hilbert space
  by $\mathcal H_\rho=L^2(\Omega;X_\rho)$.
  The initial data are projected to the band. This contracts every
  $X_\rho$ norm; we keep the original $\|v\|_\rho$ on the right of
  the estimates. The projections converge strongly to the original data.

  \emph{Ordinary coefficients and their existence.}
  Give interval $i$ its own amplitude $z_i$ and write
  \[
    \mathcal U(T;\boldsymbol z)v
    =\sum_{\boldsymbol r\in\N_0^{\mathcal F}}
    \boldsymbol z^{\boldsymbol r}U_{\boldsymbol r}v.
  \]
  These are ordinary power-series coefficients, without factorials
  incorporated in their definition. At fixed smoothing and finite inventory,
  put $v_{\max}=\sup_t\sum_i\sigma_i^2\mathbf1_{I_i}(t)
  \Gamma_\epsilon(0)<\infty$.
  On Gaussian degrees at most $n$, multiplication by the instantaneous
  field has norm at most $2\sqrt{n+1}\sqrt{v_{\max}}$.
  The free phase has norm at most $e^{\lambda h/2}$ in $X_\lambda$,
  and a phase insertion $(u,w)\mapsto(0,Wu)$ costs at most its
  unweighted multiplication norm divided by $\lambda$.
  Thus, on $\max_i|z_i|\le z_*$ the total order-$n$ phase is bounded by
  \begin{equation}\label{E:interval-entire-majorant}
    e^{\lambda T/2}
    \frac{(2z_*\sqrt{n+1}\sqrt{v_{\max}}T/\lambda)^n}{n!}
    \|v\|_\lambda .
  \end{equation}
  The series of these bounds converges for every finite $z_*$.
  It justifies the entire vector series and its absolutely convergent
  energy double series. This existence bound may depend on the full
  inventory and smoothing; it is not used as a uniform estimate.

  Set
  $Q_{\boldsymbol r,\boldsymbol q}
  =\langle U_{\boldsymbol r}v,U_{\boldsymbol q}v
  \rangle_{\mathcal H_{1/T}}$.
  Both vectors use the same Gaussian fields. Independence is between
  distinct colors, not between the two copies in this inner product.
  For real $s$, substitution $z_i=s$ gives the exact identity
  \begin{equation}\label{E:interval-energy-splits}
    [s^{2k}]\E\|\mathcal U(T;s)v\|_{1/T}^2
    =\sum_{|\boldsymbol r|+|\boldsymbol q|=2k}
    Q_{\boldsymbol r,\boldsymbol q}.
  \end{equation}
  Reflection of Gaussian color $i$ multiplies $U_{\boldsymbol r}v$
  by $(-1)^{r_i}$. Hence $Q_{\boldsymbol r,\boldsymbol q}=0$ unless
  $r_i+q_i=2k_i$ for every $i$, where $\sum_i k_i=k$.
  If $k_i=0$, both coefficients are obtained by setting $z_i=0$;
  this removes that field exactly. There are at most $k$ active colors.
  For fixed $\boldsymbol k$, the number of splits is
  $\prod_i(2k_i+1)\le3^k$.

  For this fixed $\boldsymbol k$, truncate the Hermite degree of color $i$
  at $N_i=2k_i$. A word contributing to $U_{\boldsymbol r}$ has at most
  $r_i\le2k_i$ insertions of that color. Starting in its vacuum, none of
  its intermediate degrees exceeds $2k_i$. The same holds on the other
  copy. Thus these cutoffs preserve the two selected vector coefficients
  exactly, including creation followed by annihilation. There is no
  Hermite-degree limit in this coefficient calculation.

  \emph{The split determines which norm costs are present.}
  Put $\lambda=k/T$. All fields are centered; the following names describe
  their appearances in the coefficient, not their Gaussian means:
  \[
    \begin{array}{c|c|c}
      k_i & (r_i,q_i)&\text{method}\\ \hline
      1 &(2,0)\text{ or }(0,2)&\text{mean term on one copy}\\
      1 &(1,1)&\text{one insertion on each copy}\\
      \ge2 &r_i+q_i=2k_i&\text{Cauchy on each copy where used}.
    \end{array}
  \]
  Call the second class $\mathcal C$. For the other active colors set
  \[
    \omega_i=k_i/h_i,\qquad
    d_i=2\sqrt{2k_i+1}\sqrt{R_\alpha},\qquad
    \varrho_i=\frac{\delta_a\omega_i^{2-a}}{\sigma_i d_i},
    \qquad a=\alpha/2.
  \]
  Put $\mathcal A_r=\{i\notin\mathcal C:r_i>0\}$ and
  $\mathcal A_q=\{i\notin\mathcal C:q_i>0\}$, restricting to active colors.
  On either copy write $\mathcal A$ for its corresponding set.
  Its unused amplitudes are evaluated at zero.
  In particular, a split $(2,0)$ supplies no field or cost on the second
  copy. Use the Cauchy circles $|z_i|=\varrho_i$ for $i\in\mathcal A$.

  With the colors in $\mathcal C$ set to zero, write $P_z(t,s)$ for this
  background evolution. Its phase weight is
  \[
    \Lambda(t)=\lambda+\sum_{i\in\mathcal A}
    \omega_i\mathbf1_{I_i}(t).
  \]
  This is a bounded deterministic operator on the Hilbert space containing
  all the retained Gaussian factors. Its input may depend on the very
  fields present in the background. No conditional independence between
  successive background segments is assumed.
  Lemma~\ref{L:interval-contractions}, tensored with all other color
  factors, gives for every $\rho>0$ the sandwich coefficient
  $D(t)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal A}|z_i|\sigma_i d_i
  \mathbf1_{I_i}(t)$.
  On every constant segment,
  \[
    D(t)\le\delta_a\sum_{i\in\mathcal A}
    \omega_i^{2-a}\mathbf1_{I_i}(t)
    \le\delta_a\Lambda(t)^{2-a}.
  \]
  The last inequality uses $2-a>1$.
  Lemma~\ref{L:interval-complex} bounds each segment by
  $100e^{2\Lambda(t)\Delta t}$ in its own phase norm.
  At most $k$ colors have at most $2k$ endpoints, so there are at most
  $2k+1$ such segments. Also
  \[
    \int_0^T\Lambda(t)\ud t
    \le\lambda T+\sum_{i\in\mathcal A}\omega_i h_i\le2k.
  \]
  The norm changes, including the initial and final ones, cost at most
  \begin{equation}\label{E:interval-copy-metric}
    D_{\mathcal A}:=\prod_{i\in\mathcal A}
    (1+k_i/(\lambda h_i)).
  \end{equation}
  Only an ending interval can cause a loss. Its single ending is counted
  once, even if other intervals overlap it or end simultaneously.

  \emph{The simultaneous single-insertion coefficient.}
  Let $m=|\mathcal C|$ and let $J_i(u,w)=(0,\sigma_iW_i^\epsilon u)$,
  with the stated finite cutoffs. Regard the background amplitudes as
  fixed and give colors in $\mathcal C$ amplitudes $y_i$.
  Dyson expansion relative to $P_z$ shows that the coefficient of
  $\prod_{i\in\mathcal C}y_i$ is
  \begin{equation}\label{E:interval-cross-cube}
    \sum_{\pi}\int_{D_\pi}
    P_z(T,t_{\pi_m})J_{\pi_m}
    P_z(t_{\pi_m},t_{\pi_{m-1}})\cdots
    J_{\pi_1}P_z(t_{\pi_1},0)v\,\ud\boldsymbol t,
  \end{equation}
  where $\pi$ runs over permutations of $\mathcal C$ and
  \[
    D_\pi=\{\boldsymbol t\in
    \prod_{i\in\mathcal C}(I_i\cap[0,T]):
    t_{\pi_1}<\cdots<t_{\pi_m}\}.
  \]
  Indeed, a degree-one appearance of every $y_i$ uses exactly $m$
  insertions and a permutation of the $m$ distinct colors. Each ordered
  Dyson integral has coefficient one. The $D_\pi$ partition the cube up
  to null diagonals. Thus the sum in~\eqref{E:interval-cross-cube} is a
  single integral of the time-sorted word over that cube, without a
  multiplier $m!$ or $1/m!$. If $m=0$, it means $P_z(T,0)v$.

  Before $J_i$ occurs, color $i$ is in its vacuum: the background and
  all other insertions act identically on its Gaussian factor.
  Lemma~\ref{L:interval-contractions} therefore bounds this insertion
  from the other color factors into the enlarged phase space by
  \[
    \sigma_i\sqrt{R_\alpha}\Lambda(t_i)^{(\alpha-2)/2}
    \le\sigma_i\sqrt{R_\alpha}\lambda^{(\alpha-2)/2}.
  \]
  Previously created colors are part of the remaining tensor factor in
  that lemma. No estimate for annihilation on an occupied color is used
  at a single-insertion step.

  Apply these bounds sequentially along a word. Splitting background
  segments at the $m\le k$ insertion times gives at most $3k+1$ pieces.
  Their exponential costs still multiply to at most $e^{4k}$ and their
  fixed constants to at most $100^{3k+1}$. Each metric change still occurs
  just once along the word, so its entire metric budget is
  $D_{\mathcal A}$, not a new power of that budget at each insertion.
  Minkowski over the cube consequently bounds its vector coefficient by
  \begin{equation}\label{E:interval-cross-vector}
    C^kD_{\mathcal A}\|v\|_\lambda
    \prod_{i\in\mathcal C}
    \sigma_i\sqrt{R_\alpha}\lambda^{(\alpha-2)/2}h_i .
  \end{equation}
  This holds uniformly on all the background Cauchy circles.

  \emph{Cauchy on the remaining variables and the exact inventory sum.}
  The vector in~\eqref{E:interval-cross-vector} is holomorphic in the
  remaining amplitudes. For the first copy Cauchy's vector formula gives
  the additional factor $\prod_{i\in\mathcal A_r}\varrho_i^{-r_i}$;
  on the second copy it gives $\prod_{i\in\mathcal A_q}\varrho_i^{-q_i}$.
  This is Cauchy extraction of two vector coefficients, followed by
  Cauchy--Schwarz between them, not Cauchy applied to a squared norm.
  Since $\|w\|_{1/T}\le k\|w\|_\lambda$ and
  $\|v\|_\lambda\le\|v\|_{1/T}$, the bound for
  $|Q_{\boldsymbol r,\boldsymbol q}|$ has a further factor $k^2$.
  Absorb it, the $3^k$ splits and the segment constants into $C^k$.
  The combined metric factor is $D_{\mathcal A_r}D_{\mathcal A_q}$.
  Its exponent for color $i$ is the number of positive entries in
  $(r_i,q_i)$: one for a mean singleton, at most two for a higher color.

  For any color estimated on circles, the two exponents add to $2k_i$.
  Since $d_i^2\le12k_iR_\alpha$,
  \[
    \varrho_i^{-2k_i}
    \le(CR_\alpha\sigma_i^2h_i^{4-\alpha})^{k_i}
    k_i^{-(3-\alpha)k_i}
    \le\frac{(CR_\alpha\sigma_i^2h_i^{4-\alpha})^{k_i}}
    {(k_i!)^\nu}.
  \]
  After $\sigma_i^2\le c h_i^{-\alpha_0}$, the resulting local weights,
  with a common per-total-order constant understood, are
  \[
    \begin{array}{c|c}
      \text{case}&\text{weight in energy}\\ \hline
      (2,0)\text{ or }(0,2)&R_\alpha h_i^\beta
      (1+1/(\lambda h_i))\\
      (1,1)&R_\alpha\lambda^{\alpha-2}h_i^{2-\alpha_0}\\
      k_i=l\ge2&R_\alpha^l h_i^{\beta l}
      (1+l/(\lambda h_i))^2/(l!)^\nu .
    \end{array}
  \]
  The first metric factor occurs on only one copy; the last costs at
  most one factor on each copy. These weights increase with $h_i$:
  their expanded powers are $\beta,\beta-1,p$ and
  $\beta l,\beta l-1,\beta l-2$, all positive.
  There are two orientations of the singleton mean and one cross split.
  If their weights are $m_i$ and $c_i$, then
  $2m_i+c_i\le3(m_i+c_i)$.
  Higher degree $l$ has at most $2l+1\le3^l$ splits.
  Thus, summing the split choices costs only a constant per total order,
  and the singleton weight is the sum of the two types in the table.
  Substituting $h_i\le LT/i$ and $\lambda=k/T$ therefore extracts
  $C^kR_\alpha^kT^{\beta k}$, leaving $v_i$ at $k_i=1$ and
  $b_{i,l}$ at $k_i=l\ge2$ from Lemma~\ref{L:inventory-product}.

  There is no further multinomial factor: for nonnegative weights
  $w_{i,0}=1$, ordinary multiplication of power series gives exactly
  \[
    \sum_{\sum_i k_i=k}\prod_i w_{i,k_i}
    =[z^k]\prod_i\left(\sum_{l\ge0}w_{i,l}z^l\right).
  \]
  Apply this identity to the sum in~\eqref{E:interval-energy-splits},
  using the two singleton alternatives together in $v_i$.
  Lemma~\ref{L:inventory-product} now proves
  \eqref{E:interval-coefficient-bound} at the finite cutoffs.

  \emph{Identification and summation.}
  Keep positive smoothing, finite inventory and a fixed coefficient.
  The finite-degree multiplication bound used in
  \eqref{E:interval-entire-majorant} is uniform in mode and band cutoffs.
  On finite-coordinate Gaussian vectors the omitted annihilation tail is
  zero and the creation tail tends to zero by its omitted pointwise
  variances and dominated convergence. Density in each finite degree
  extends this strong convergence. Closedness identifies the limit with
  actual multiplication by the measurable smoothed field.
  Fourier projections converge strongly and commute with free evolution.
  Dominated time integration thus identifies every fixed Duhamel word.
  The selected coefficients of the local Hermite truncation were already
  equal to those untruncated words. The full actual series is constructed
  separately using~\eqref{E:interval-entire-majorant}, choosing a sufficient
  degree cutoff anew at each order. A fixed-degree truncated propagator
  is not identified with the full evolution.
  The entire majorant gives convergence uniformly on compact amplitude
  sets. The coefficients estimated above are therefore those of the
  actual finite-interval model, with no zero-smoothing limit at this step.

  The nonuniform number $v_{\max}$ was used only for this identification.
  The uniform coefficient bound contains no such number. Its constants
  come from $\delta_{\alpha/2}$, the fixed phase bound, the inventory
  lemma and $L,c$; covariance enters only through $R_\alpha^k$.
  The zero-order energy is at most $e\|v\|_{1/T}^2$.
  Put $x=CR_\alpha T^\beta$ and $Y=e^{\nu x^{1/\nu}}\ge1$.
  Since $\nu>1$, $\sum_{k\ge0}x^k/(k!)^\nu\le Y$.
  Summing the coefficient bounds in absolute value gives
  \[
    \E\|\mathcal U(T;1)v\|_{1/T}^2
    \le(e+Y-1)\|v\|_{1/T}^2\le eY\|v\|_{1/T}^2.
  \]
  Absorbing $\nu C^{1/\nu}$ into the parameter-only constant proves
  \eqref{E:interval-phase-bound}. This proves
  Proposition~\ref{P:interval-coefficients}.
\end{proof}
